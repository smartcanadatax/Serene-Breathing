Breathing for Anxiety Attacks — What to Do Right Now

Serene Breathing · May 2026 · 6 min read

← Back to Blog

⚠️ If you are in a medical emergency, call 911. Chest pain, severe difficulty breathing, or symptoms you cannot distinguish from a cardiac event require immediate medical attention. This article is for educational purposes and is not a substitute for professional medical or mental health care. If anxiety attacks are frequent or severely impacting your life, please speak with a doctor or therapist.

If you are in the middle of an anxiety attack right now, skip straight to the technique below. If you are reading this to prepare for the next time, everything you need is here.

Do This Right Now

  • Sit down or lean against a wall. Do not try to push through it while standing.
  • Inhale through your nose for 4 slow counts. Fill your belly, not just your chest.
  • Exhale through your mouth for 8 slow counts — twice as long as the inhale. Make a soft "haah" sound if that helps.
  • Repeat this 6–10 times without rushing.
  • Remind yourself: "This is anxiety. It is uncomfortable but not dangerous. It will pass."

The extended exhale is the fastest physiological brake available to your nervous system. Within 60–90 seconds of doing this, your heart rate will begin to slow.

Why Anxiety Attacks Happen

An anxiety attack is your body's threat-response system activating without a physical threat present. Adrenaline floods your system, your heart rate surges, your muscles tighten, and your breathing becomes rapid and shallow. This cascade is designed to prepare you to run from a predator — but when triggered by a thought, a situation, or nothing identifiable at all, it feels terrifying and out of control.

One of the mechanisms that makes anxiety attacks worse is hyperventilation. When you breathe fast and shallow, you exhale more CO₂ than your body produces. Lower CO₂ causes blood vessels to constrict slightly, reducing blood flow to the brain — which causes dizziness, tingling in the hands and face, and a feeling of unreality. This makes you more frightened, which makes you breathe faster, which drops your CO₂ further. It is a vicious cycle that breathing directly interrupts.

The Main Techniques — In Order of Urgency

1. Extended Exhale (Fastest-Acting)

This is the technique to use in the moment of an anxiety attack. The long exhale directly stimulates the vagus nerve — your body's primary calming pathway — and begins to slow your heart rate within seconds.

Extended Exhale Breathing (4-8)

  • Inhale through your nose for 4 counts
  • Exhale through your mouth for 8 counts — slowly, like breathing through a straw
  • Repeat 6–10 times, or until you feel the peak intensity begin to ease

If counting feels hard during the attack, simplify: just make every exhale twice as long as your inhale. That is the only rule that matters.

2. Physiological Sigh (When the Chest Feels Tight)

During an anxiety attack, the chest often feels tight and it is difficult to take a full breath. The physiological sigh — a double inhale — is specifically designed to re-inflate lungs that are partially collapsed due to shallow, rapid breathing.

Physiological Sigh

  • Inhale through your nose until you can't take in any more air
  • At the top, take one more short sniff in through your nose — just a little more
  • Exhale everything slowly through your mouth — take as long as you can
  • Repeat 3–5 times

This technique works especially well when you feel unable to catch your breath or when your chest feels locked. The double inhale physically opens the airways that shallow breathing has partially closed.

3. Box Breathing (To Stabilise After the Peak)

Once the worst intensity has passed — usually within 5–10 minutes — box breathing helps you stabilise and prevent a second wave. Its equal-count pattern gives the mind something precise to focus on, which reduces the catastrophic thinking that can prolong an attack.

Box Breathing (4-4-4-4)

  • Inhale for 4 counts
  • Hold for 4 counts
  • Exhale for 4 counts
  • Hold for 4 counts
  • Repeat 6–8 times

Use this after the peak passes, or as a preventative technique if you feel anxiety building before an attack begins.

What to Say to Yourself During an Attack

Your internal narrative during an anxiety attack matters enormously. The instinctive response — "something is terribly wrong," "I can't cope," "make it stop" — intensifies fear and extends the attack. These phrases help interrupt that pattern:

  • "This is anxiety. My body is working exactly as designed — just at the wrong time."
  • "This is uncomfortable, but it is not dangerous. I have survived this before."
  • "Anxiety attacks always pass. The peak is less than 10 minutes."
  • "I do not need to stop this — I just need to breathe through it."

After the Attack — What to Do

In the 30–60 minutes after an anxiety attack, your body is in a recovery phase — cortisol and adrenaline are still elevated and you may feel exhausted, shaky, or emotionally raw. This is normal and will pass.

  • Drink water — dehydration worsens anxiety symptoms
  • Do 5 more minutes of slow breathing to help cortisol clear faster
  • If safe to do so, take a short walk — gentle movement helps process residual adrenaline
  • Avoid caffeine for the rest of the day
  • Be kind to yourself — anxiety attacks are not a character flaw or a sign of weakness

Building Resilience — Daily Breathing Practice

Breathing during an attack is reactive. But a daily breathing practice is preventative — it raises your baseline parasympathetic tone, which means your nervous system is less likely to tip into an attack in the first place.

Research consistently shows that even 5 minutes of daily slow breathing (around 5–6 breaths per minute) meaningfully improves heart rate variability, lowers baseline anxiety, and reduces the frequency and intensity of anxiety episodes over weeks. It is one of the most evidence-based, accessible tools available — with no side effects.

When to seek professional help

Breathing techniques are a valuable tool for managing anxiety, but they are not a substitute for professional care. If anxiety attacks are frequent (more than once a month), significantly impact your daily life, or if you are avoiding situations because of fear of an attack, please speak with a doctor or licensed therapist. Effective treatments including CBT and medication are available and can make a profound difference.
